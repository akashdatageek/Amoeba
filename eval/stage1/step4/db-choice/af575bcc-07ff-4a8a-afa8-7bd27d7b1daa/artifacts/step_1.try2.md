## Takeaways
1. **Benchmarks are vendor-skewed and cannot decide this.** The two sources with measurable numbers are a Postgres vendor whitepaper (S4) and an aggregator that shows no methodology (S1). Neither gives the hardware or configuration needed to reproduce it. Our prototype is the deciding evidence.
2. **Ingest and analytics point in different directions.** The claims retrieved say MongoDB leads on simple insert and bulk-write throughput [S1]. They say PostgreSQL leads on GROUP BY aggregation [S1] and on JSONB latency in the EDB test [S4]. A dashboard product is mostly tenant-scoped aggregation, so the analytical side matters more than raw inserts.
3. **Our load is modest.** The peak of 5,000 queries/min across all tenants is 5,000 / 60 = 83.3 QPS (calculation). That is far below the quoted 10,000–35,000 TPS-class figures [S1]. Raw engine throughput is unlikely to be the bottleneck. p95 per query over 50 x 200 GB = 10,000 GB of raw data, index and storage design, and cost are more likely to decide.
4. **None of the benchmarks tests tenant isolation.** They do not cover 50 tenants at this data size, noisy neighbours, per-tenant partitioning or sharding, tenant-scoped indexes, or EU/US residency. These all need the prototype.
5. **Version and date drift.** The EDB test appears old [S4], with the date unverified. The MongoDB 8.0/9.0 gains are second-hand [S1]. Re-run on current PostgreSQL and MongoDB versions, on the managed tiers we would buy (RDS, Aurora, Cloud SQL, AlloyDB and Atlas).

Handoff to the cost-model steps: use these results only as a direction. Size compute from the prototype's measured p95 at 83.3 QPS (calculation above). Use the measured PostgreSQL and MongoDB storage ratios, and fall back to the analyst brief's default ratios if they are not measured. Those defaults are brief settings, not web figures.
