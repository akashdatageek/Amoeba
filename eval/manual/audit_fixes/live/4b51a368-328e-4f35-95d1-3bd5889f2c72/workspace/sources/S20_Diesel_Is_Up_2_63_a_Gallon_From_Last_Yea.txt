Title: Diesel Is Up $2.63 a Gallon From Last Year: What the EIA Fuel Data Means for Your Routes

### Diesel Is Up $2.63 a Gallon From Last Year. Every Mile Costs More.

### What the EIA Fuel Data Means for Landscaping and Construction Routes, and Where to Find the Miles You Can Cut

![Infographic: the U.S. average retail diesel price was 6.38 dollars a gallon the week of September 28, 2026, up 70 percent from 3.75 dollars a year earlier, and California averaged 8.18 dollars, the highest of any region.](img/diesel-price-fall-routes.webp)

Same truck. Same 30 gallon tank. Same diesel pump on the way out of town.

At the end of September 2025, filling it at the national average price cost about **$113**. At the end of September 2026, the same fill-up cost about **$191**. Nothing about the truck, the route, or the crew changed. The price of a gallon did.

For landscaping and construction companies, fuel is one of the few costs that changes every week, and this year it has changed more than most owners have ever seen. The federal data shows how much, and it also shows why the miles your trucks drive matter more right now than they did a year ago.

Fall is a driving season. Cleanups, hauling, dump runs, and equipment moves all put more miles on the trucks. Every one of those miles now costs more than it did when this year's prices were set.

### What the Weekly Numbers Say

The U.S. Energy Information Administration surveys retail fuel prices every week. For the week of **September 28, 2026**, the most recent at the time of writing, the national average retail price of on-highway diesel was **$6.382 a gallon**. A year earlier, the week of September 29, 2025, it was **$3.754**. That is an increase of **$2.628 a gallon**, or about **70 percent** (*EIA, Weekly Retail Gasoline and Diesel Prices, September 2026*).

Most of that increase came this year. Diesel averaged **$3.477** the first week of January 2026 and **$3.897** on March 2. One week later, on March 9, it was **$4.859**, a jump of nearly a dollar in seven days. After easing to **$4.578** in early July, it climbed again, reaching **$6.529** the week of September 21, the highest weekly average of the year so far (*EIA, weekly U.S. No. 2 diesel retail prices, 2026*).

Gasoline trucks are not spared. The national average for regular gasoline was **$4.465** the week of September 28, 2026, up **$1.347** from a year earlier (*EIA, September 2026*).

### Where You Operate Matters

The weekly survey also breaks prices out by region. For the week of September 28, 2026, average diesel prices were (*EIA, September 2026*):

* **California:** $8.181
* **West Coast:** $7.357
* **Central Atlantic:** $6.531
* **Midwest:** $6.526
* **New England:** $6.510
* **Rocky Mountain:** $6.407
* **Gulf Coast:** $5.955
* **Lower Atlantic:** $5.953

Even the cheapest regions are well above where the national average stood a year ago.

### What EIA Expects Next

EIA's Short-Term Energy Outlook, released September 9, 2026, forecasts that retail diesel will average **$5.07 a gallon for all of 2026** and **$4.40 in 2027**, and attributes the elevated prices to a tight global distillate market (*EIA Short-Term Energy Outlook, September 2026*).

Read the 2026 number carefully. It is a full-year average that includes January and February, when diesel was under $4. It does not mean prices will be at $5.07 next month. The 2027 forecast is lower, but forecasts change, and nobody should build next season's bids on the hope that fuel gets cheap again.

### What a Mile Costs Now

Here is an example, not survey data. Say a crew truck pulling a loaded trailer averages 10 miles per gallon. At the September 28, 2026 national average, every mile costs about **64 cents** in diesel. A year earlier, it was about **38 cents**.

Now say that truck drives an extra 10 miles a day that it did not need to, from backtracking, a forgotten part, or a stop out of order. Over five days a week for a 30 week season, that is 1,500 miles and 150 gallons, about **$957** at today's average, compared with about **$563** at last year's. For one truck.

### Fuel Is Not Even the Biggest Cost of a Mile

The crew is in the truck for every one of those miles, and under federal wage rules, "travel from job site to job site during the workday, must be counted as hours worked" (*29 CFR 785.38*).

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports that production and nonsupervisory employees in landscaping services earned an average of **$27.71 an hour in August 2026** (*BLS Current Employment Statistics, August 2026, not seasonally adjusted*).

Back to the example. Ten extra miles at an average route speed of 25 miles per hour is 24 minutes. For a three person crew, that is 1.2 crew hours, or about **$33 in wages** at the BLS average, against about **$6.38 in diesel**. Even at this year's prices, the labor in an unnecessary mile costs several times the fuel.

That is the good news hidden in high fuel prices. The same work that cuts fuel cost, removing miles that did not need to be driven, cuts the larger labor cost at the same time.

### Where to Find the Miles

**1. Get a baseline.** Know how many miles each truck drove last week, and how many of those were between properties.

**2. Compare planned to driven.** Look at the route a crew was given next to the route it actually drove. Backtracking and out-of-order stops show up immediately.

**3. Cluster the stops.** Group properties by area, and put the dump site or supply house on the way rather than after the last stop.

**4. Cut the extra supply runs.** A missing part that costs one 20 mile round trip now costs about $1.28 in diesel and much more in crew time, by the example math above.

**5. Watch trucks after hours.** Take-home trucks and weekend use add miles that are easy to miss.

**6. Re-price with today's number.** Contracts and bids written when diesel was under $4 may not cover the fuel. For snow contracts and next season's renewals, consider a fuel adjustment clause tied to a published index like EIA's weekly price.
